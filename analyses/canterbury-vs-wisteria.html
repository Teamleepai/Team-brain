Canterbury vs. Wisteria

A structural read of two California Life Plan communities against the Windsor benchmark — where the money actually goes, what the fee sheets don't say, and the one clause that matters more than every price on either page.

Sources: 2026 fee schedules, both communities Canterbury eff. 07·01·2026 Wisteria eff. 02·01·2026

Bottom line

Given that one spouse is expected to need care soon, the contract structure is worth more than the entrance-fee difference — and the two communities are not structured the same way. Wisteria is a Type A Life Care contract, where the monthly fee stays roughly flat if a resident moves into assisted living or memory care. Canterbury's own sheet offers a 5% discount in Assisted Living, the Courtyard and Care Suites — which is fee-for-service with a courtesy discount, not life care.

On a realistic memory-care scenario, that single difference runs $80,000–$105,000 per year in Canterbury's disfavor. It overwhelms the roughly $250,000–$300,000 Canterbury saves on the way in. If the spouse who needs care can pass Wisteria's health underwriting, Wisteria is the better structure for this family. If she cannot — and Type A communities underwrite hardest precisely because they carry the care risk — that decision gets made for you, and Canterbury becomes the answer by default.

Everything below is the evidence for that, plus the eleven things I'd want answered in writing before anyone signs.

Two things that would change this conclusion

And the gate on all of it: health underwriting

Type A communities underwrite health and finances hardest, precisely because they are assuming the care liability. A prospective resident with an existing dementia diagnosis or significant care needs may be declined for independent living, required to enter assisted living directly at a different price, or offered a modified contract that strips the life-care benefit — which would eliminate the entire advantage above. This needs to be established before you spend another hour on floor plans.

Ask Wisteria first, in writing: given this specific diagnosis and care profile, does she qualify for the standard Type A contract in independent living — and if not, what exactly is offered instead, and at what price?

The caveat that undoes this: Choice 1's advantage depends entirely on the 80% actually being paid. That is the subject of the next section, and it applies to both communities.

The repayment problem nobody advertises

Both communities market a large refund. Canterbury calls it an "80% Re-occupancy Benefit." Wisteria calls it a "Repayable Entrance Fee." Read both names literally — the money is conditioned on the unit being re-occupied or resold, and neither sheet states a date certain.

This is a known and active problem in California. The legislature has addressed it twice. SB 939, effective 2017, forces interest to accrue on unpaid repayments — 4% after 180 days from vacancy, 6% after 240 days, compounding annually thereafter — and requires providers to make a good-faith effort to re-occupy. Critically, it also requires contracts to disclose the average and the longest time the provider has taken to resell a unit over the last five calendar years. That disclosure is your single best diagnostic, and you are entitled to it.

AB 1983 is moving through the legislature right now, and it targets Wisteria's model

AB 1983 (Rubio, 2025–26 session, passed the Senate committee stage as of June 2026) expands the definition of a "repayable contract" to cover fees repaid "based on the sequential order in which repayable contracts are terminated" — that is, a queue. Under a queue model your estate is not waiting for your unit to resell; it is waiting for everyone ahead of it in line to be paid first. The fact that the legislature is writing a bill specifically about this practice tells you how often it goes wrong.

Ask both, in writing: is repayment triggered by resale of my specific unit, or by position in a queue? What were the average and longest repayment times in the last five years?

Wisteria cannot answer that question, and that is the point

Wisteria opened in 2025 and is still filling 486 residences. It has no five-year resale history to disclose, because it has no history at all. Your estate's repayment would depend on the resale market for a large, brand-new, still-filling community — and on Wisteria's cash position during fill-up, which is the most capital-hungry phase of a CCRC's life. Canterbury, at 187 licensed beds and decades of operation, can produce a real track record. Make it.

Material

A California appellate court let a Spieker/Continuing Life community bill residents for its own legal costs

In Johnson v. Stoneridge Creek Pleasanton CCRC LLC, a resident sued the community, owner Spieker Senior Development Partners and operator Continuing Life over alleged violations including the Consumer Legal Remedies Act and the Elder Abuse Act, and challenged the community passing anticipated litigation costs to residents through fee increases. The appellate court held the Health & Safety Code permits a CCRC to include "reasonable projections" of litigation expense in monthly fees. Residents can therefore fund the defence of claims brought against the community — including their own neighbours' claims. This is now settled California law and applies across the sector, but it is live precedent involving this specific ownership group.

Ask Wisteria: is Continuing Life LLC the operator here, and does the monthly fee methodology include projected legal expense?

Dealbreaker

The $950,000 is not $950,000

Selling the house triggers capital gains. With the $500,000 married-couple exclusion and a long-held California home with a low basis, the taxable gain can still be substantial. On a $950,000 sale with, say, a $150,000 basis: $800,000 gain, less the $500,000 exclusion, leaves $300,000 taxable. At federal long-term rates plus the 3.8% net investment income tax plus California income tax, that is plausibly $70,000–$100,000 gone — so the real proceeds are nearer $850,000–$880,000. That is the difference between clearing Canterbury's Mira Costa comfortably and not clearing it at all.

Get the actual adjusted basis from their CPA before committing to any number. Improvements over the decades raise basis and reduce the bill.
Material

An entrance fee is not a house, and the tax treatment differs sharply

They are converting an appreciating asset with a stepped-up basis at death into a contract right that does not appreciate, cannot be borrowed against, generates no mortgage interest deduction, and carries no Proposition 13 protection. That may well be the right trade for the care and the freedom from maintenance — but it should be a decision, not a surprise.

On the other side of the ledger: Type A contracts are the ones that qualify for the prepaid-medical-expense deduction, typically 30–40% of the non-refundable portion of the entrance fee and of monthly fees, subject to the 7.5%-of-AGI threshold, and claimable only in the year paid. Wisteria's Type A structure likely qualifies. Canterbury's, if it is genuinely a 5%-discount fee-for-service arrangement, likely does not — which quietly widens the real cost gap further in Wisteria's favour. Confirm with their CPA; this is worth real money and is routinely missed.

You've said the monthly fee is covered by the annuity, Social Security and book royalties. For today, that's true. The issue is that these four lines move at different speeds.

Is the annuity fixed or inflation-adjusted? If fixed, model the crossover year — the point where the fee exceeds covering income — and make sure there is a reserve sized to bridge from there.
